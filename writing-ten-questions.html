Should We Still Teach Academic Writing? Ten Questions We Urgently Need to Ask

You start typing an email. Five words in, the machine offers the rest of the sentence, and it says exactly what you were about to say. You press tab and think nothing of it. Nobody calls that cheating. Now put the same moment inside a student’s essay. Is the sentence still theirs? I sat with that question one evening and found nine more waiting behind it.

One afternoon this summer, a friend and I got into a long conversation about teaching, in particular academic writing. I am a computational sociologist. He is a machine learning researcher. I started it with a thought experiment. Suppose I assign an essay. A student gives the prompt to an AI, gives it the reading list, and asks it to draft the essay. The student edits, polishes, and submits. Now there are two branches. In one, something makes me suspicious. There is a procedure for that branch. It is a misconduct case, I am required to report it, and the machinery of academic integrity takes over.

In the other branch, I notice nothing. The essay is good. The grade is fair, by everything I can see. The deadline was met and everyone moves on. We talked for hours and settled nothing. What we produced instead was questions.

Here are ten of them. None of them has a settled answer yet, and that is precisely why they deserve our attention now, while the norms are still being written.

1. If a rule can only be enforced by chance, is it still a rule?

Our ability to detect AI writing is probabilistic at best, and it gets worse every term, even with the detect system.

2. In the undetected case, what exactly was lost, and who lost it?

If we want students to take the harm seriously, we owe them a precise description of it.

3. What was the essay measuring in the first place?

AI did not break the essay. It broke the link between the artifact and the process.

4. Is writing the only way to train thinking, or just the way we inherited?

My instinct says that writing trains critical thinking, that the struggle to build an argument is where the thinking gets built. I believe this. I was formed by it. But I have to admit I hold it as an inherited conviction, not an established law. If the goal is judgment and the essay is one training method for it, we should at least be able to ask whether it is the only method, or simply the one our discipline has always used.

5. Can you learn to judge writing without producing it?

This was my friend’s contribution, and it is the one I keep chewing on. In machine learning, you can train a model to distinguish good from bad with labeled examples, and learning to judge is often easier than learning to generate. So he asked: if a student can reliably tell a strong argument from a weak one, and explain why, has the essay’s purpose been achieved without the essay? Or is producing the thing a precondition for judging it? I notice that art history students learn to see without painting. I also notice that every writing teacher I respect disagrees. I do not know who is right.

6. When the machine completes the sentence I was about to write, who wrote it?

Anyone who writes in an AI-enabled editor knows this moment. You type a few words, the tool proposes the rest, and sometimes the proposal is exactly what you meant. Not close. Exact. If I accept a sentence that says precisely what I intended to say, it feels dishonest to call it the machine’s sentence, and slightly delusional to call it mine. Our whole vocabulary of authorship assumes that intention and production happen in the same person. That assumption is now optional.

7. Where does assistance end and substitution begin?

Spellcheck, then grammar suggestions, then translation, then autocomplete, then a drafted paragraph, then a drafted essay. It is a smooth slope with no visible joint.

8. Can we ban in the essay what we encourage in the code?

In computational social science we teach writing and programming side by side. In programming, working with an AI assistant is rapidly becoming normal professional practice, and refusing to teach it would arguably shortchange students. So the same student, in the same degree, in the same week, may be encouraged to use AI in one file and forbidden to use it in another. There may be a principled reason the two cases differ. But if we cannot articulate it clearly, students will reasonably conclude that the distinction is not principled at all.

9. What would an assessment look like that AI cannot hollow out, and what would it cost?

There are honest answers here. Oral exams. Live analysis of data nobody has seen before. Writing done in the room. Assessing the process rather than the product. Each one restores the link between performance and person. And each one has a price: they are harder to scale, harder on anxious students, and often hardest on exactly the students who already face the most barriers, including those working in a second language. Anyone who says the fix is simple has not priced the fix. The real question is which costs we are willing to pay, and who pays them.

10. What are we modelling when we use these tools ourselves?

Teaching a practice we are all still learning may simply be the condition of this decade. It is uncomfortable, and I would rather be honest about the discomfort than perform a confidence I do not have.
