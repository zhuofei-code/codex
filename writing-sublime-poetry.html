Sublime Poetry: What a Museum Tour Taught Me About Human-Centred AI

We don’t need machine-perfect precision, or more information. We need warmth, breath, and room to breathe — the gust of wind you feel in a Monet before you’ve read a single word.

Zhuofei Lu · 24 June 2026 · ~5 min read

Follow on LinkedIn Read the original on LinkedIn →

I began with a small, slightly unfair experiment on my own family. Before we set off for the museum, I set them up with an AI guide — Doubao, ByteDance’s assistant. You point your phone at a painting, and it talks: who made it, when, what the symbols mean, how it connects to a dozen other works. Ask a follow-up, and it answers at once, having quietly searched the literature while you were still forming the question. On every metric I can name as a researcher — speed, accuracy, breadth, recall — it outperforms any human guide I have ever hired. It does not get tired. It is never stumped.

And yet, after one museum, my family asked me to find a human guide for the next.

I am a sociologist; I cannot let a finding like that go. Why?

The Temperature of Attention

The answer, when it came, was not about information at all. A few days earlier, at the National Gallery of Art in Washington, a young woman who had studied art and history walked us through the galleries. She did not deliver a firehose of facts. She spoke slowly, left silences, watched our faces to see what had landed, laughed at her own asides, met our eyes. At one point she stopped us in front of Monet’s Woman with a Parasol — Madame Monet and her son, out on a windy hill. Look, she said — he barely gives her a face. There is almost no realistic detail to “know.” And that is exactly the point: what the painting offers is not information but sensation — sun pushing through the green parasol, the veil and the long dress lifting in the wind, the grass all leaning one way. You are not meant to read the picture; you are meant to feel the breeze. Which, it struck me later, was precisely what she was giving us, too.

We left feeling not informed but accompanied. The word my family reached for was 温度 — warmth, literally “temperature.” The AI had given us everything except the temperature — and the breath — of another person paying attention. And this is worth being precise about, because “warmth” can sound like a soft excuse for a slower tool. It isn’t. Meaning in a museum is not transferred; it is co-produced — in a shared glance, a pause that lets awe arrive, the small social permission to feel something in public. The guide was not a better database. She was a person sharing her attention with ours, and that turns out to be the whole event.

When the Machine Took the Likeness

Later, among the Impressionists and those who came after, I found myself in front of the very thing that explains our present anxiety. Why did European painting turn, in a single generation, away from faithful representation and toward light, colour, and feeling — the Impressionists’ broken light, Van Gogh’s restless skies? The usual story is also the truest one: the camera arrived. For centuries a painter’s livelihood had been likeness — the aristocrat’s portrait, the church’s narrative wall. Then a machine learned to capture a likeness in seconds, and the commission migrated from the painter to the photographer. Painting did not die. It relocated. It went to the one place the machine could not follow: the interior, the subjective, the felt.

This is, almost exactly, our story now. A technology has become startlingly good at something we had quietly assumed was ours — drafting, summarising, analysing, explaining a Raphael to a tourist. So we panic. We fear for our jobs; we wonder, more privately, what we are for. I study the world of work for a living, and I will not pretend that fear is irrational. But the long view offers something steadier than reassurance. Every time a new machine has made human beings doubt their own worth, the artists and the philosophers have turned in the same direction for an answer — back toward the human. The Renaissance answered the rigid medieval order by re-centring human dignity, reason, and beauty. The Enlightenment and the Romantics answered the smoke of industry with conscience and feeling. The question keeps its shape; only the machine in the room changes. And the answer keeps its shape too: put the human back at the centre.

To Show His Hand

Which brings me to Raphael — and a twist I didn’t expect. His workshop was, in its way, a Renaissance AI. He ran a studio of dozens; he copied his own designs at scale with pricked cartoons and pounced charcoal — a five-hundred-year-old photocopier — and had the engraver Marcantonio turn his compositions into prints that spread across Europe like a feed. Reproduction, automation, reach: the workshop did everything we now fear AI will do to creative work.

So look at what Raphael did in 1515. He sent a red-chalk drawing of two nude figures — made entirely by his own hand — across the Alps to Albrecht Dürer in Nuremberg. Dürer kept it, and wrote on the sheet that Raphael had sent these figures “to show his hand.” Not a finished painting. A drawing. Because the grand works were made by many hands — and could be — but a drawing was one hundred percent his. When the most celebrated artist in Rome wanted another master to see who he really was, he did not send the workshop’s output. He sent his own hand.

The workshop could reproduce. It could not be Raphael.

So here, at least for now, is where I would draw the line. AI can be the workshop. Let it do the pricking and the pouncing — the searching, the drafting, the tireless first explanation. What it cannot be is the hand, or the temperature of a person in the room turning to you to see whether the painting has moved you too.

Human-centred AI, then, is not a posture of resistance. It is an act of deliberate curation: deciding, on purpose, which work we hand to the machine and which we keep human — the warmth, the breath, the eye contact, the meaning made between people.

Let the machines have the workshop; let us keep the hand. That is what this writing is about: not the technology itself, but the social worlds we build around it — and whether they stay worthy of human beings. The Met named its Raphael show Sublime Poetry. And sublime poetry, in the end, is the part of being human that no machine can ghost-write.
